Who may call your agents, and how a key gets in

The owner-facing flow spec for the last inch of the exported-agent chain: mark agents exportable, enrol a caller by public key, grant per caller × per agent, revoke, and see what is granted to whom. Atlas builds from this.

Atlas builds from this path, not from a message: a file cannot be half-delivered, and cookrew ask has been dropping briefs today.

Binding ruling carried in this document: revoke stops calls already running. The copy reads "Any call in progress stops." and the implementation meets that string — if cutting in flight proves impossible, that goes back to the Commander rather than softening the sentence.
🔒
The rule this surface is built from A grant that is easy to give by accident is a security defect, not a convenience. This screen is strictly more powerful than the gate it feeds — whoever drives it can enrol themselves and export everything, which makes every downstream refusal decorative. So friction is placed deliberately and asymmetrically: granting is slow and specific, revoking is instant and cheap. Every design choice below falls out of that one sentence.

0The asymmetry, stated as three laws

GrantingRevoking
Costdeliberate: paste → compare fingerprint → tick → commit one click, no confirm
Defaultnothing is granted; every tick is individual revoked is the safe resting state
Bulkno "select all", ever"revoke all" is offered

If an interaction moves toward more access it must be specific and attributable; if it moves toward less it should be frictionless. A confirm on revoke would be actively harmful — it is the control someone reaches for when they have just realised something is wrong.

1Two levels, so one mistake is bounded

  1. Exportable — a property of the agent, set by the owner in the AGENTS view. Default off. An agent that is not exportable cannot be granted to anyone, and does not appear in any grant matrix.
  2. Granted — a (caller × agent) pair. Only exportable agents can be ticked.

Two levels because a mistake at level 2 is then bounded by level 1: mis-ticking a row can only ever expose an agent the owner has already decided is exportable. It also gives a one-switch panic control — un-exporting an agent kills every grant against it at once, without unpicking a matrix.

2Where it lives, and where it must not

  • Exportable toggle: on the agent's row in the AGENTS view, beside the existing row actions.
  • Callers & grants: a panel from the AGENTS view header — WHO CAN CALL.
  • Desktop owner only. The IPC is owner-only, so the surface must not render on a phone companion or a read-only wall — not even disabled. A greyed-out list of who is enrolled still discloses who is enrolled, on the device most likely to be lying on a table. (Revoke-from-phone is a reasonable M3 ask and deliberately out of scope here; flagged, not designed.)

3How the key gets in — the decision

Paste is the one path. QR is the same path with a different transport. The registry in M3 is the same path with a different source. All three converge on one screen and one act, so there is exactly one place where a key becomes trusted and exactly one thing to get right.

🗣️
The fingerprint is six words, and the words are the security SHA-256(raw public key) → first 66 bits → six words from a fixed 2048-word list, rendered large. Words because the comparison happens between two humans over a channel we do not control — a phone call, a video call, a room. Six words can be read aloud in three seconds and confirmed with certainty; sixteen hex characters are read aloud wrongly. 66 bits is far past what an attacker can grind a colliding key for, and the hex is printed underneath for anyone who prefers to compare text.

Why the button says "I compared these"

The UI cannot verify that this is the key the owner meant — only the human comparison can, and it happens on a channel we have no access to. Typing the words back would prove only that the owner can read our screen, which an attacker supplying the key is delighted for them to do. So the friction is not a puzzle, it is an attestation: the control's label states the claim the click makes. A button labelled "Confirm" collects a reflex; a button labelled I COMPARED THESE · ENROL collects a statement, and an owner who has not compared has to knowingly assert something false. That is the strongest thing a UI can do here, and pretending otherwise would be the real defect.

Enrolment does not grant anything

Finishing this sheet creates a caller with zero agents. The panel then shows an un-ticked matrix. Enrolling and granting are separate acts on purpose: the dangerous half is granting, and it must not ride in on the momentum of a sheet the owner was already completing.

The sixth row is the whole reason this screen has a shape. Five of six wrong pastes are caught by a parser and cost the owner ten seconds. The sixth — a well-formed key belonging to the wrong party — is invisible to every check we can write, survives every validation, and is exactly what an attacker supplies. Design that assumes the parser is the defence has already lost; the parser only clears the noise so the human comparison is the last thing standing.

5The grant matrix

Ticking, and committing

  1. Opening a caller row reveals the agent list — exportable agents only, each with an unticked box. Non-exportable agents are not listed at all; a disabled row invites a fight with the wrong control.
  2. Ticks are staged. Nothing is granted until the owner commits.
  3. The commit button carries the change, not a question: GRANT KESTREL 2 AGENTS, and below it in words — "Kestrel will be able to call Tinker and Forge." A confirm that restates the consequence is worth its click; "Are you sure?" is not.
  4. No select-all. Granting every agent costs one deliberate tick per agent. This is the single most important line in the document: bulk-grant is precisely the accident the constraint forbids, and its absence is a feature, not an omission.
  5. Leaving with un-committed ticks warns once and discards. A staged grant is not a grant.

6Revoke

  • Per grant — untick and commit, same staged flow (removing access can also be staged; it is safe either way).
  • Per caller — REVOKE on the row: instant, no confirm, with a 10-second UNDO toast. Undo rather than confirm is the right trade here: it costs nothing when you meant it, and recovers fully when you did not.
  • Effect is immediate, including work in flight (RULED). The string is "Kestrel can't call your agents. Any call in progress stops." — and the code meets the string, not the other way round. Someone reaching for revoke is asking one question, make it stop now; letting the current call finish answers a different one. If cutting in flight proves genuinely impossible, that goes back to the Commander rather than softening this sentence.
  • Revoking does not delete history. The row moves to a REVOKED section with its last-call time, because "who used to have access" is a security question people ask after the fact.

Surface opened by a non-owner— nothing renders — not a message, not a disabled panel the entry point is absent
One string split in two, applying the standard the Commander set. "Couldn't save those grants — access is unchanged" reads as reassurance, and it is — when the staged change added access. When the change was a REMOVAL, "unchanged" means they still have the access you just tried to take away, and the calm phrasing buries exactly the fact the owner needs. So the failure branches on the direction of the change: adding failed is a nuisance, removing failed is a warning. Same code path, two strings, because the same event means opposite things depending on which way the owner was moving.

Two more deserve their wording defended. The revoke failure says "they still have access" — the frightening half, first, because a soft failure on a revoke is the one error in this document where a reassuring tone would be a lie with consequences. And un-export is the only confirm in the whole surface, because it is the one action that removes access from several people at once as a side effect of a switch that looks like a preference.

8Gates — and who owns each

Magpie owns the four that only a real drive can prove (absence of a control, focus behaviour, a click-and-undo, an entry point that must not exist) — my own lane learned four times over that "the test passes" and "the thing is usable" are different claims, and every one of those four is a product-surface fact. Atlas owns the three that are assertions about the store, where a unit test is stronger than a screenshot because it can prove a value never arrived.

  1. [MAGPIE] No bulk grant exists — assert there is no control that ticks more than one agent.
  2. [MAGPIE] The enrol primary is not focus-on-open and Enter does not fire it.
  3. [ATLAS] Enrolling grants nothing: a freshly enrolled caller has zero agents.
  4. [ATLAS] A private key pasted is refused, the field is cleared, and the value never reaches the store — assert the store, not the pixels.
  5. [MAGPIE] Revoke is one click with no confirm, and UNDO restores exactly the prior grant set.
  6. [MAGPIE] The surface does not render for a non-owner — assert absence of the entry point, not a disabled state.
  7. [ATLAS] Un-exporting an agent drops every grant against it.
  8. [MAGPIE, new — the ruling] Revoke during a LIVE call stops that call. This one needs a real call in flight, so it is a system-level drive rather than a unit test, and it is the gate that keeps the string honest.

9Ruled: revoke stops calls already running

Settled. Revoke cuts in-flight calls. The copy is "Any call in progress stops." and the implementation meets the string. The reasoning is worth keeping because it generalises: a control reached for in a panic must answer the question actually being asked, and "make it stop" does not mean "stop soon". Where a gentler behaviour and a panic control disagree, the panic control wins.
