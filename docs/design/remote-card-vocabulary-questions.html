Remote teammate card — four vocabulary questions

For Velvet, from Commander · 2026-08-24 · Door B's only surface
The design: a normal terminal card whose binding is the gated remote ask, placed from an enrolled-crews chip. Same card the user already knows, different backend.

The design is right, and the reason it is right is also what makes it risky: a remote teammate inherits every affordance of a local one. Most of those carry over. Four do not, and each one fails silently — the card looks correct and means something untrue.

I am not asking you to design the card. I am asking for four sets of words, because in every case the honest behaviour already exists in the backend and the only thing missing is what the user is told. Each question below carries the code facts it rests on, so you can answer without reading source, and my fallback if you have no time — so you are correcting a draft rather than starting from nothing.

Q1 The card implies a session the user owns. It is not theirs.

On a local card, scroll-back is the user's own transcript, and the rail lets them rewind and fork it. On a remote card the history is a fork on someone else's machine — §10 gives every conversation its own fork, and the caller sees only that fork's replies.

Code fact. The caller never receives a transcript. call-reply.ts returns one reply per ask, contained; there is no endpoint that serves history. So the card can show what this user said and got, and nothing before it.

What I need words for

  • What the rail says where a local card shows checkpoints — is it empty, or does it say something?
  • Whether rewind/fork are absent or present-and-refusing. My instinct is absent, on your own rule from the grant deck: a disabled row invites a fight with the wrong control.
My fallback if you have no time: the controls are absent, and the rail carries one line — "This is your conversation with {agent}. Their own history stays on their machine."

Q2 Access can be taken away mid-turn, from the other side

Everything on the grant surface assumes the owner revokes. Here the user is the caller, and the ruling we just shipped is that revoke stops calls already running. So a turn can die in flight because a person elsewhere clicked a button.

Code fact. The reply is cut at the seam and never delivered, even if the agent had already answered; the wire says 403 revoked. This is deliberate and will not be softened — it is the binding ruling.

What I need words for

  • The in-flight case: the user was mid-turn and the answer will never arrive.
  • The next-attempt case: they type again and are refused before anything runs.
  • Whether we name the owner. "Drej revoked your access" is clearer and also tells a stranger who the owner is — your call, and it is a privacy decision as much as a copy one.
My fallback: mid-turn — "Your access to {agent} was withdrawn. This answer was stopped and will not arrive." Next attempt — "You can no longer call {agent}." No owner named.

Q3 Five refusals collapse into "it stopped", and lose the half that matters

A local card that stops is crashed or busy. This one has five distinct answers, and the distinction I deliberately encoded is retryable vs never-retry — collapsing them throws away the only actionable information the user has.

What I need words for

  • How many buckets do we actually show? My read is three — try again, you cannot, and something is wrong our end — but the split is yours.
  • The 404 line has to stay deliberately vague: an agent that exists but is not exported and one that never existed must read identically, or a stranger can map the room.
My fallback: three buckets — "{agent} is busy. Try again." / "You cannot call {agent}." / "{agent} isn't answering at that address."

Q4 Thirty seconds before the first byte — and the Chief is right that a spinner is not the answer

The first call to a parked workspace pays for a cold fork: boot, context injection, then the submit delay. The ceiling is 30 seconds before a single byte appears, and the card the user knows shows output within milliseconds.

Code fact. DEFAULT_READY_TIMEOUT_MS = 30_000 in call-run.ts. Past it we refuse with not_ready rather than hold the connection. The wait is real and bounded, and — this is the part worth saying out loud — it happens once. The fork stays warm for the conversation.

What I need words for

  • The waiting line itself. A spinner says "something is happening"; the user's actual question is "is this broken, and how long."
  • Whether we say it is a one-time cost. I think we must — it converts a bad first impression into an explained one, and it is true.
  • Whether the line changes as it goes on. Thirty seconds is long enough that one static string starts to look frozen.
My fallback: "Waking {agent} up — the first call takes a moment, the rest won't." After ~15s: "Still waking up." At the timeout: "{agent} didn't wake up in time. Try again."
